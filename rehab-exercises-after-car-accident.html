Rehab Exercises After a Car Accident: Why Rest Alone Isn't Enough

By the Cedar Chiropractic Team · 4 min read · Back to blog

A lot of people leave the ER or urgent care after a crash with the same advice: take it easy, rest up, see how it goes. And for the first couple of days, that's sensible.

The trouble is what "see how it goes" can turn into. Three weeks later you're still sore, you've stopped doing the things you used to do, and the muscles around the injury have quietly gotten weaker.

What rest does, and doesn't do

Early rest gives irritated tissue a break. That part is reasonable. But muscles that aren't asked to work get weaker surprisingly fast, and an injured neck or back is often guarded and stiff on top of that. The body protects the sore area by tensing up around it, and over time that protection becomes a habit the body doesn't know how to drop.

Moving gently, within what your body tolerates, tends to do the opposite. It encourages blood flow, keeps joints from stiffening, and helps the muscles remember their job.

What rehab looks like in practice

It's not a gym session, and it isn't punishing. Early on it's often small and slow: gentle range-of-motion work, easing the neck through a comfortable arc, or low-back movements that don't flare things up. Later it builds toward strengthening the deep muscles that stabilize the spine, the shoulder blades, and the hips.

A few examples of the sort of movements that can show up (these aren't a prescription for you, since your exam decides what's right):

  • Gentle chin tucks to wake up the small stabilizers at the front of the neck
  • Shoulder-blade squeezes for the muscles between the shoulder blades
  • Pelvic tilts and similar low-back movements while lying down
  • Short, easy walks that slowly get longer

How much, and how often

Short and regular usually beats long and rare. A few minutes a day, done with good form, tends to do more for an injured neck or back than one big session on Sunday that leaves you sore for three days. We'll tell you what to do, how many, and how it should feel. If you're not sure, ask. A two-minute check of your form at the next visit saves a lot of wasted effort.

If you're nervous about moving

That's more common than people admit. After a crash, many people get wary of their own body and start avoiding movements that might hurt. It makes sense. The trouble is that fear can turn into a habit of guarding, and the guarding keeps things stiff. Starting small and gentle, with someone watching, takes some of that fear out of it.

Worth knowing: If an exercise makes your pain sharply worse, sends tingling down an arm or leg, or leaves you feeling worse hours later, stop and tell us. More is not better here. The right amount is the one your body can handle that day.

How it fits with the rest of care

Think of the pieces as a sequence. Adjustments help joints move. Ultrasound and e-stim can help calm the muscles that have clamped down. Then rehab and corrective exercise teach the body to hold that improvement so it doesn't slide back. Skip the last piece, and some people find they're in the same spot a few months later.

Rehab is also the part you do on your own, at home, which is why it's worth getting right. We show you the movements, watch how you do them, and adjust as you improve.

Tracking progress

Pick two or three everyday things and watch them: how far you can turn your head, how long you can sit before you shift, whether you can reach the top shelf. Those tell you more than a pain number. Write them down every week or two. When something stalls, that's useful information, and it's the kind of thing we want to hear about at your visit.

Don't wait until you feel perfect

Some people assume they should wait until the pain is gone to start moving. It's usually closer to the reverse, though the timing and amount are individual.
