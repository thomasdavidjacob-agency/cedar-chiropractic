Injured in a Crash? How Good Records Support Your Claim

By the Cedar Chiropractic Team · 4 min read · Back to blog

One of the first things an insurance company wants to know after a crash is usually this: when did you first get checked out? Not what happened, not who was at fault. Just when you first saw someone.

That one question shows why records matter. This is general information, not legal advice, and your own insurer or attorney can tell you what applies to your claim.

Why gaps get noticed

Say your neck starts hurting three days after a crash, and you don't see anyone for six weeks. To an adjuster reading a file, that gap leaves room for questions. Did something else happen in between? Is this really from the accident? You and I know bodies don't always cooperate with a schedule, but a file just shows dates.

Visits that are close together and consistent tell a simpler story. That's not about gaming anything. It's about having an honest, clear record of what happened to you.

What you can keep on your own

Most of this takes five minutes a day, and it's the stuff clinics can't capture for you.

  • A symptom log. Date, where it hurts, how bad (one to ten), what you couldn't do.
  • Photos. Vehicle damage, the scene, and any bruising, including seat-belt marks.
  • The claim number and the name of every adjuster you talk to, with dates.
  • Receipts for anything related to the crash: tow, rental, prescriptions, copays.
  • Work notes. Days you missed, tasks you couldn't do, anything your employer said.
Worth knowing: Be honest in your notes, including the good days. Records that read as truthful are more useful than records that read as dramatic. If you felt great on a Saturday, write that down too.

What a good log looks like

Nothing fancy. Something like: "Oct 3. Neck 6 out of 10 in the morning, 4 by afternoon. Couldn't turn left to check traffic. Slept about five hours, woke twice. Missed my morning walk." Two or three lines a day is plenty. The details, like what you couldn't do, say more than the number does.

What we keep on our end

From your first visit, we write down what you tell us about the crash, what we find on exam, what care we provide, and how you're responding. We note when something improves and when it doesn't. Those notes follow you through your care, and with your permission we can share records with your insurer or your attorney when they ask.

We can't tell you what a claim is worth, or how it will turn out. That's for the insurer and, if you have one, an attorney. What we can do is keep accurate, consistent records of your care.

When life gets in the way

Things happen. A kid gets sick, work blows up, the insurer takes two weeks to call you back. A gap in your records isn't always avoidable, and it isn't always a problem, but it's easier to explain when there's a note. If you miss a stretch of care, jot down why and tell us at your next visit so it's written down accurately instead of reconstructed months later.

And hang on to everything until your claim is fully closed. Ask your insurer or attorney how long to keep it after that.

Keep your appointments, and tell us when you can't

Missing visits and stopping care early are the two ways a record gets patchy. If something comes up, call and reschedule rather than letting it slide. And if you feel better and want to wind down, say so. A good plan adjusts to you.
