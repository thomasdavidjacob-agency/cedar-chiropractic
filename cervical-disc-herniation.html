Cervical Disc Herniation: Signs, Causes & Non-Surgical Options

By the Cedar Chiropractic Team · 5 min read · Back to blog

It starts as a nagging ache at the base of your neck. A week later your thumb and index finger go numb when you hold the steering wheel, and you keep shaking the hand out like it fell asleep. That pattern is common with a cervical disc herniation, and it is one of the more common reasons people in Milwaukie and Oregon City call us asking about cervical disc herniation treatment.

What the disc is doing in there

Between each pair of neck bones sits a disc, a tough ring with a softer, jelly-like center. Think of a jelly doughnut. When the outer ring weakens or tears, some of the filling can push outward. If it pushes toward a nerve root, the nerve gets irritated, and that is where the trouble starts. The nerve is not just sore in your neck; it complains along its whole route.

Not every bulging disc causes symptoms, which surprises people. Plenty of necks have disc changes and never hurt. The ones that get attention are the ones pressing on something.

Signs people tend to describe

  • Neck pain that moves into the shoulder blade, upper arm, forearm, or fingers
  • Tingling, pins and needles, or numbness in the hand
  • A weaker grip, or dropping things you would normally hold fine
  • Pain that gets worse when you tip your head back or turn toward the sore side
  • Relief when you rest your hand on top of your head (some people stumble onto this by accident)

None of these is a diagnosis. Muscle strain, joint irritation, and other things can look similar from the outside, which is why the exam matters more than the guess.

How a neck disc gets irritated

Sometimes there is a clear moment: a car crash, a fall, lifting something awkward overhead. After a rear-end collision, a disc that was already a little worn can be pushed past its limit, and symptoms may not appear for days. If that sounds like your situation, our pieces on whiplash after a car accident and pain that shows up days later cover why the delay happens.

Other times there is no moment at all. Years of wear, genetics, and the ordinary stresses of living in a body can thin a disc slowly until one normal morning it finally complains.

Worth knowing: a lot of neck-and-arm pain that people call a "pinched nerve" turns out to be muscle and joint irritation rather than a disc. Only an exam can sort out which one you have.

Non-surgical options to ask about

Many people with a neck disc problem are first managed without surgery, and that is usually where a conservative provider starts. Depending on what your exam shows, a plan at Cedar may draw on a few things:

  • Spinal adjustments to restore motion in stiff neck joints, with gentler low-force techniques available if you prefer them
  • Cervical traction, a gentle sustained stretch intended to ease pressure on the disc and nerve (here is more on how cervical traction works)
  • Spinal decompression, a controlled, non-surgical stretch on a treatment table, if your exam suggests it is worth trying
  • Rehab and corrective exercise so the muscles around the neck can hold things steady once the pain settles

Whether any of those fit you depends on your history and findings. Nobody can honestly promise that a disc will shrink or that symptoms will disappear. What care can often do is lower the irritation so you can sleep, turn your head, and use your hand again.

If the disc trouble followed a crash, it also helps to know how the pieces fit together. Traction, decompression, and rehab in whiplash recovery walks through that, and our auto injury care page explains how crash-related visits work.

When it cannot wait

Most neck and arm pain is not an emergency. A few things are. Go to the ER or call 911 for any of these:

  • Weakness in an arm or hand that is getting worse quickly
  • Trouble walking, balance that suddenly feels off, or legs that feel heavy
  • New loss of bladder or bowel control
  • Severe neck pain with fever, or after a serious fall or crash
  • Sudden severe headache, trouble speaking, or facial drooping

For everything short of that, an exam is the sensible next step. If you are not sure how to get started, our new patient page lays out what the first visit looks like, or you can contact us and ask.
