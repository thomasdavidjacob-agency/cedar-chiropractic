Electrical Muscle Stimulation (E-Stim): How It Eases Pain

By the Cedar Chiropractic Team · 4 min read · Back to blog

The first time someone feels e-stim, they often laugh. It's a tapping, buzzing, pins-and-needles kind of feeling, and it isn't what most people picture when they hear the word "electrical." E-stim therapy is a common companion to chiropractic care for sore, tight backs and necks, and here's how it works and what the sensation is about.

What it is

Electrical muscle stimulation, e-stim for short, uses a small device that sends mild electrical pulses through pads placed on the skin over or near a sore area. You lie comfortably, the pads go on, and the intensity is turned up until you notice it. Then it's adjusted so that it's comfortable. The strength is yours to call. Our ultrasound and e-stim page gives the overview.

Does it hurt?

It shouldn't. Strong, yes, sometimes. Painful, no. If it ever stings or feels like too much, tell the person running it, because settings can change in seconds. A few people with sensitive skin notice some redness where the pads were, which usually fades.

Why electricity would calm anything

Two different ideas are at work, depending on the type of setting used.

With one kind, the gentle signals travel along sensory nerves and may compete with pain signals heading to the brain, so the area can feel quieter for a while. That's a widely described explanation, not a proven certainty for every person.

With another, the pulses are set to make a muscle contract and release in a rhythm. For a muscle that's been clenched around an irritated joint, that pumping action may help it let go. Depending on your exam, care may include either kind, or neither.

Why muscles get that tight to begin with

A muscle that clamps down usually has a reason. Maybe a joint underneath it is stiff, maybe you slept badly on it, maybe it's been holding one position through a long day of driving around Portland. Whatever started it, a tight muscle gets sore, a sore muscle tightens more, and that loop can run for weeks. E-stim is one way to interrupt the loop long enough to do something about the cause.

What it's good for, and what it isn't

E-stim tends to come up for muscle tightness and soreness in the neck, shoulders, and low back. It's a way to take the edge off so the rest of the visit goes better. It doesn't fix a stiff joint, correct why a muscle got weak, or replace movement. If that sounds like a lot of "it doesn't," that's the honest part. People who lie on a table and feel better afterward sometimes assume the table did all of it. Usually it didn't.

Worth knowing: Relief from e-stim can fade within hours or a day for some people. That isn't failure. It means the underlying reasons for the tightness still need attention.

Who should say something first

Mention these before any pads go on:

  • a pacemaker, defibrillator, or other implanted electronic device
  • pregnancy
  • epilepsy or a heart rhythm problem
  • broken or irritated skin in the area
  • numb areas where you wouldn't feel a setting that's too strong

Pads also generally aren't placed across the front of the neck or over the chest. Those are common precautions, not a complete list, and your health history is what we go by.

What about the units sold at the drugstore?

Small stimulation units are easy to buy, and some people use them without trouble. Still, ask before you stick pads on a neck or back that nobody has examined. Where the pain actually comes from decides whether electrical stimulation is a sensible idea, and a pad on the wrong spot, or on someone who should avoid it, doesn't help anything.

Putting it with other care

E-stim and warmth-based treatment often show up in the same visit. Therapeutic ultrasound in chiropractic care explains the other half of that pairing. Once a muscle quiets down, the next steps are spinal adjustments for joints that aren't moving and rehab exercises so it holds.
