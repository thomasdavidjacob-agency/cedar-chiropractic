Cervical Traction for Neck Pain & Disc Herniation: How It Works

By the Cedar Chiropractic Team · 4 min read · Back to blog

Someone tells you they'd like to gently stretch your neck, and your first thought is probably a fair one: why, and is that safe? Cervical traction is one of the quieter tools a chiropractor can reach for when a neck is sore, stiff, or sending odd sensations into the shoulder and arm. Here's the plain version, for neighbors in Milwaukie and around the Portland area who've heard the word and aren't sure what it means.

What traction actually is

A steady, controlled pull that separates the bones of the neck by a small amount. That's the whole idea. You're supported lying down or sitting, a head support or harness holds you, and the pull is applied for a set time and then released. Nobody twists your head. Depending on the setup it can be a mechanical device or done by hand, and the amount of pull is set to what your neck tolerates that day. Our cervical and lumbar traction page covers the basics.

Why a disc or a nerve might care

Between each pair of neck bones sits a disc, and right beside it is a small opening where a nerve leaves for the shoulder, arm, and hand. When a disc bulges or herniates, or the joints around it swell and tighten, that opening can get crowded. People describe the result as neck ache plus pain, tingling, or numbness running down one arm.

Easing the squeeze, even slightly, may take some irritation off the nerve and give the neck room to settle. That's the reasoning, and it's why traction is mostly considered for disc-related neck problems. If you want the longer story on the condition itself, cervical disc herniation: signs, causes, and non-surgical options goes through it.

The vocabulary trips people up. "Bulge," "herniation," and "slipped disc" get used as if they mean the same thing. A bulge means the disc has pushed outward. A herniation means some of the soft center has pushed through the tougher outer wall. And the disc doesn't slip out of place like a drawer coming off its track. Neither word tells you how much something hurts, either. Plenty of people have disc changes on imaging and no symptoms at all, which is one reason the exam matters more than the picture alone.

What a session tends to feel like

Most people call it a gentle stretch. Some find it a relief, some find it a little strange. It shouldn't hurt. If it pinches, stirs up arm symptoms, or leaves you light-headed, say so and the pull gets backed off or stopped. That feedback matters more than any number on a dial. Afterward the neck may feel looser, or just a bit tired.

When traction is the wrong call

Not every neck should be stretched. A recent fracture, an infection, some bone-weakening or joint-instability problems, and certain vascular conditions are reasons to skip it. Your exam is how those get ruled in or out, and we'd rather rule traction out than use it by default.

Some symptoms need a medical setting, not a treatment table: sudden weakness in an arm or leg, trouble walking, new loss of bladder or bowel control, or a severe headache unlike any you've had. Get urgent care for those.

Making room is only step one

On its own, traction mostly creates space. What happens with that space depends on the next part: getting stiff joints moving, and strengthening the small muscles that hold your head up so the neck doesn't slide back into old habits. Depending on your exam, care may pair traction with spinal adjustments and rehab and corrective exercise. Nobody can tell you ahead of time how many visits that will take.

Spinal decompression is a close relative that's more often used lower down. We cover it in lumbar traction and spinal decompression for low-back pain.

From the front desk: If your neck trouble started after a collision, the timeline looks a little different, and so does the paperwork.
