Why Does Pain Show Up Days After a Car Accident?

By the Cedar Chiropractic Team · 4 min read · Back to blog

Tuesday morning, someone taps the back of your car on McLoughlin. You get out, you're shaken but fine, you exchange insurance info and go to work. By Friday you can't look over your shoulder without wincing.

If that sounds familiar, you're not imagining it. This is one of the most common stories we hear.

Adrenaline is a very good painkiller

During and right after a crash, your body dumps stress hormones into your system. They sharpen your attention and dull pain, which is useful when you're trying to get out of traffic safely. They also wear off, usually within hours. Once they do, whatever got strained starts to make itself known.

Then the inflammation builds

When muscles and ligaments are stretched past their limit, the area doesn't swell up instantly. Inflammation develops over the next day or two. Tissue that felt fine at the scene gets puffy, tender, and stiff, and the muscles around it tighten up to protect it. That guarding is part of why a neck can feel fine on Tuesday and locked on Thursday.

Sleep matters here too. You lie still for hours, the tissue stiffens, and the first thing you notice in the morning is how little your neck wants to move.

Some people also notice that the pain moves. Day one it's the neck. A few days later it's between the shoulder blades or down in the low back, as the body shifts load around to protect the sorest spot. That's common too.

Where it tends to show up

The neck is the classic spot, but delayed soreness can land almost anywhere your body was holding on during the crash:

  • Neck and upper shoulders, often with a headache at the base of the skull
  • Low back, especially where the seat belt and the seat held you in place
  • Mid-back between the shoulder blades
  • Hands, wrists, and shoulders if you were gripping the wheel

Tingling, a heavy arm, or dizziness can show up late as well. Those are worth mentioning to whoever examines you. Arm symptoms can sometimes involve a herniated disc in the neck.

"Delayed" doesn't mean "imagined"

Patients sometimes apologize when they call. "I know it's been a few days, and I felt fine at first, so I feel silly." Please don't. Feeling okay and then not okay is so common that we'd be more surprised if everyone hurt right away.

The flip side happens too. Some people are sore within the hour, and some never develop symptoms at all. There isn't one normal timeline, which is why we suggest watching yourself closely for the first week or so.

Worth knowing: Symptoms that get steadily worse, a severe headache, confusion, numbness or weakness, or loss of bladder control are not wait-and-see problems. Go to the ER or call 911.

Why waiting can cost you

There are two reasons we'd rather see you sooner than later. The practical one: stiff joints and guarded muscles tend to be easier to work with before the body has settled into a new, protective pattern. The paperwork one: if you do end up making an insurance claim, a gap of weeks between the crash and your first visit gives an insurer room to argue the pain came from somewhere else. A visit dated close to the accident is a cleaner record. (Your policy and your claim details govern what happens, and none of this is legal advice.)

What helps in the meantime

Move gently. Short walks are usually better than lying flat all day. Skip the heavy lifting. And write down what you're feeling each day, because patterns are useful when we sit down with you.

At the visit we'll examine your neck and back, ask how the crash happened, and talk through options. Depending on what we find, that could include ultrasound and electrical stimulation to calm irritated muscle (here's what therapeutic ultrasound involves), along with spinal adjustments and other therapies. We bill PIP directly for auto injury patients from Milwaukie, Oak Grove, Gladstone, and across Portland.
